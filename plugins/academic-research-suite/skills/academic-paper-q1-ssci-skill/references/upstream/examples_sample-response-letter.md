## Response to Reviewer #1

### Comment R1.1
> *The theoretical framework needs strengthening. SDT is mentioned
> briefly but not sufficiently developed. How exactly does each SDT
> construct (autonomy, competence, relatedness) map onto the three
> formative assessment practices?*

**Response:**
We sincerely appreciate this insightful comment. We agree that the
original theoretical framework section was insufficiently detailed. In
response, we have substantially expanded Section 4.1 (Theoretical
Foundation, pp. 6-8) to include:

1. A detailed explanation of each SDT construct and its relevance to
   formative assessment
2. A new conceptual mapping table (Table 1, p. 7) linking each SDT
   need to specific formative assessment mechanisms
3. An explicit discussion of why SDT is more appropriate than
   alternative frameworks (e.g., social cognitive theory) for this study

The expanded text includes:

*"The need for competence is supported when students receive formative
feedback that provides clear, specific information about the gap between
current performance and desired standards (Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick,
2006). This feedback enables students to calibrate their self-assessment
and adjust their learning strategies accordingly. The need for autonomy
is fostered through self-assessment practices, which position students
as active evaluators of their own work rather than passive recipients
of external judgments (Boud and Molloy, 2013). Finally, the need for
relatedness may be supported through peer assessment, which creates
opportunities for collaborative evaluation and mutual learning (Topping,
2009)."*

(See revised manuscript, Section 4.1, pp. 6-8, and Table 1, p. 7.)

### Comment R1.2
> *The sample size for the quantitative component (N = 312) is adequate,
> but the qualitative sample (N = 18) needs better justification.
> How was data saturation determined?*

**Response:**
Thank you for raising this methodological point. We have added a
detailed justification for the qualitative sample size in Section 5.2
(p. 11). Specifically:

*"Eighteen participants were selected for semi-structured interviews
using maximum variation sampling to ensure diversity in gender, academic
discipline, and self-regulation level (as measured by MSLQ scores). Data
saturation was assessed following the guidelines of Saunders et al.
(2018): after the 14th interview, no new themes emerged, and the
remaining four interviews served as confirmation. This approach aligns
with Guest et al.'s (2006) finding that thematic saturation in
relatively homogeneous samples is typically reached within 12-15
interviews."*

(See revised manuscript, Section 5.2, p. 11.)

## Response to Reviewer #2

### Comment R2.1
> *The discussion is too descriptive. Results are repeated rather than
> interpreted. Please deepen the theoretical interpretation and provide
> more substantive comparison with prior research.*

**Response:**
We agree that the original Discussion was insufficiently analytical.
We have restructured and substantially revised Section 7 (Discussion,
pp. 19-24). For each key finding, we now provide:

1. A brief restatement (1 sentence)
2. Comparison with 3-4 prior studies
3. Theoretical interpretation through SDT
4. Specific practical implication

For example, the discussion of H1 now reads:

*"The finding that instructor feedback quality significantly predicted
self-monitoring behaviors (beta = .34, p < .001) is consistent with
extensive prior research in Western educational contexts (Hattie and
Timperley, 2007; Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick, 2006). This result extends
these findings to Vietnamese higher education, where instructor-student
hierarchies may amplify the importance of feedback quality. Through the
lens of SDT, this finding suggests that high-quality feedback satisfies
the competence need by reducing uncertainty about performance standards,
thereby enabling students to engage in more accurate self-monitoring.
Practically, these results suggest that faculty development programs in
Vietnamese universities should prioritize training instructors to
provide specific, criterion-referenced feedback rather than simply
increasing feedback frequency."*

(See revised manuscript, Section 7.2, pp. 19-22.)

### Comment R2.2
> *The limitations section should discuss common method bias, as all
> quantitative data were collected via self-report.*

**Response:**
This is an excellent point. We have added a common method bias (CMB)
analysis to Section 6.1 (Preliminary Analyses, p. 15) and discussed its
implications in the Limitations section (Section 7.5, p. 23):

In Results: *"Common method bias was assessed using Harman's single-
factor test. The unrotated factor analysis yielded six factors with
eigenvalues greater than 1.0, with the first factor accounting for
27.3% of the total variance, which is below the 50% threshold (Podsakoff
et al., 2003). Additionally, a marker variable technique confirmed that
CMB did not substantially inflate the observed relationships."*

In Limitations: *"Although common method bias tests did not indicate
significant concerns, the reliance on self-reported data for all
quantitative measures remains a limitation. Future studies could
incorporate objective measures of self-regulated learning, such as
learning analytics data or behavioral observations, to complement
self-report measures."*

(See revised manuscript, Section 6.1, p. 15, and Section 7.5, p. 23.)
