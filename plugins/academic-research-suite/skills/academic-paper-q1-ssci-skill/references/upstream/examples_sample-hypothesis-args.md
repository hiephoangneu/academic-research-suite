# Sample Hypothesis Development Arguments

> Demonstrating how to build logically from theory and evidence to a
> testable hypothesis. Each example follows the three-step pattern:
> Theory → Evidence → Hypothesis.

---

## Example 1: Instructor Feedback and Self-Monitoring

### Theoretical Rationale

Self-determination theory (SDT; Ryan and Deci, 2000) posits that
individuals are more likely to engage in self-regulated behaviors when
their basic psychological need for competence is supported. In
educational settings, competence support occurs when learners receive
clear, informative feedback that helps them understand the gap between
their current and desired performance levels (Deci and Ryan, 2008).
Formative feedback, by providing specific information about strengths
and areas for improvement, serves as a primary mechanism for satisfying
the competence need.

### Empirical Evidence

Consistent with this theoretical prediction, several empirical studies
have documented a positive relationship between formative feedback and
self-monitoring behaviors. Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick (2006) proposed a
seven-principle model in which effective feedback clarifies performance
standards and facilitates self-assessment. Panadero and Jonsson (2013)
reported that students who received criterion-referenced feedback showed
significantly higher self-monitoring scores compared to those who
received normative feedback (*d* = 0.52). Similarly, in a quasi-
experimental study of 284 undergraduates, Winstone et al. (2017) found
that an intervention focused on feedback literacy significantly improved
students' self-regulation (*p* < .01, eta-squared = .09).

### Hypothesis

Based on the theoretical rationale provided by SDT and the consistent
empirical evidence, the following hypothesis is proposed:

> **H1:** Instructor feedback quality positively predicts self-monitoring
> behaviors among undergraduate students.

## Example 2: Peer Assessment and Intrinsic Motivation

### Theoretical Rationale

SDT further posits that the need for autonomy, defined as the sense
of volition and ownership over one's actions (Ryan and Deci, 2000), is
essential for intrinsic motivation. Peer assessment, which involves
students evaluating each other's work, has been theorized to support
autonomy by positioning students as active agents in the assessment
process rather than passive recipients of instructor judgments (Boud
and Molloy, 2013). When students engage in peer assessment, they are
expected to develop a sense of ownership over the assessment criteria,
thereby enhancing their intrinsic motivation.

### Empirical Evidence

Empirical support for this relationship, however, is mixed. Topping
(2009) found that peer assessment had a small but positive effect on
student motivation in a meta-analysis of 26 studies (*d* = 0.24).
Similarly, Li et al. (2020) reported that peer assessment activities
enhanced perceived autonomy among graduate students in a writing course.
Conversely, Panadero et al. (2017) found no significant relationship
between peer assessment frequency and intrinsic motivation in secondary
school students, suggesting that the mere presence of peer assessment
may be insufficient without adequate scaffolding and training.

### Hypothesis

Despite the mixed evidence, the theoretical prediction from SDT and
the majority of empirical findings suggest a positive, albeit
potentially modest, relationship. Therefore:

> **H2:** Peer assessment frequency positively predicts intrinsic
> motivation among undergraduate students.

> **Note:** Given the mixed evidence, this hypothesis will be tested
> with particular attention to potential moderating variables (e.g.,
> prior peer assessment experience, training quality).

## Example 3: Self-Assessment and Goal-Setting Accuracy

### Theoretical Rationale

The third component of SDT, the need for relatedness, is less directly
relevant to self-assessment. However, Zimmerman's (2002) cyclical model
of self-regulation provides a complementary theoretical lens. According
to Zimmerman, the self-reflection phase of SRL involves comparing
outcomes to goals and attributing results to causes. Self-assessment
requires students to engage in precisely this type of evaluative
thinking, thereby strengthening the forethought phase (goal setting)
in subsequent learning cycles.

### Empirical Evidence

Andrade (2019) conducted a comprehensive review of self-assessment
research in education and concluded that self-assessment consistently
improves the accuracy of students' performance judgments when
accompanied by clear rubrics. Yan and Brown (2017) provided experimental
evidence showing that structured self-assessment activities improved
goal-setting specificity (*F*(1, 126) = 8.73, *p* = .004, eta-squared
= .07). Brown and Harris (2014) further reported that self-assessment
training enhanced students' calibration accuracy, reducing the gap
between estimated and actual performance by approximately 15%.

### Hypothesis

Drawing on Zimmerman's cyclical SRL model and the consistent empirical
evidence, the following hypothesis is proposed:

> **H3:** Self-assessment practice positively predicts goal-setting
> accuracy among undergraduate students.

## Template: Hypothesis Argument Structure

```
### Theoretical Rationale (1 paragraph, 80-120 words)
- State the theory and its relevant proposition
- Explain the mechanism linking Variable A to Variable B
- Cite the original theorists

### Empirical Evidence (1-2 paragraphs, 120-200 words)
- Present 3-5 supporting studies
- Note any contradictory evidence
- Indicate the weight of evidence (consistent, mixed, limited)

### Hypothesis (1-2 sentences)
- State as: "[Variable A] [positively/negatively] [predicts/relates to]
  [Variable B] among [population]."
- Number clearly: H1, H2, H3...
- Add a note if evidence is mixed
```
