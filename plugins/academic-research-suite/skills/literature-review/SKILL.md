# Literature Review and Framework Builder

## Define the review decision

1. Identify the topic, purpose, research domain, phenomenon, unit and level of analysis, context, time horizon, focal outcome, candidate variables, and intended deliverable.
2. Translate raw keywords into concepts, synonyms, acronyms, adjacent constructs, and plausible relationships. Do not assume that similarly named constructs are equivalent.
3. If the user supplies variables, classify each as a possible antecedent, outcome, mediator, moderator, boundary condition, control, or rival explanation. Treat this classification as provisional until theory and evidence support it.
4. When the focal outcome, unit, or causal ordering is genuinely underdetermined, present two or three candidate formulations and ask the user to choose. Do not force every keyword into one model.
5. Choose the review mode:
   - Use an integrative, theory-building review by default when the goal is explanation or framework development.
   - Use a systematic review only when the question, inclusion logic, databases, screening process, and reporting can support reproducible coverage.
   - Use a scoping review for broad concept, evidence, and boundary mapping.
   - Use a critical review when the main purpose is to challenge assumptions or reconcile competing theoretical positions.
6. Ask only for missing information that would materially alter the scope or model. Otherwise, state assumptions and continue.

## Design and document the search

1. Build concept blocks for the focal phenomenon, constructs, theories, mechanisms, contexts, and outcomes.
2. Create database-specific Boolean searches with synonyms, spelling variants, acronyms, and exclusion terms where justified.
3. Search both the contemporary frontier and foundational literature. Use recent syntheses to orient the field, then trace backward to seminal theories and forward to later tests, extensions, and critiques.
4. Prioritise authoritative scholarly databases, publisher pages, DOI records, official repositories, and original papers. Use discovery engines to locate sources, not as final evidence when stronger records are available.
5. Record the database or platform, exact query, filters, date searched, result count when available, and selection decisions.
6. Use backward and forward citation chaining for influential and closely matched studies.
7. Never describe a review as exhaustive or systematic when source access, search coverage, screening, or reproducibility is incomplete. Label a web-only search as bounded and explain its limitations.
8. For broad or publication-bound reviews, work in auditable stages: scope and protocol, search and export, deduplication and screening, extraction and appraisal, then synthesis. Never imply that later stages are complete after only a search plan or discovery pass.
9. For a publication-bound systematic review, version the protocol, consider registration where appropriate, use independent or duplicate screening when feasible, prespecify the critical-appraisal method, reconcile extraction disagreements, and prepare the applicable reporting checklist and flow diagram. Recommend meta-analysis only when studies and effect measures are substantively comparable.

## Verify and appraise the evidence

1. Verify every source used in the output against an authoritative record. Check authors, year, title, outlet, DOI or stable URL, and retraction or correction status when material.
2. Read enough of each source to verify the specific claim. Abstract-only access may support only claims explicitly stated in the complete abstract and must be labelled as such. Do not infer detailed findings from a title, abstract fragment, citation snippet, or another paper's summary.
3. Separate conceptual arguments, empirical associations, causal evidence, methodological contributions, and review-level conclusions.
4. Appraise design quality, construct validity, identification, sample and context fit, temporal ordering, robustness, and inferential limits.
5. Treat journal quartiles, rankings, policies, and database coverage as time-sensitive and verify them when they affect inclusion or evaluation.

## Synthesize rather than catalogue

Organise the literature around debates, mechanisms, contradictions, and boundary conditions, not one paragraph per author.

1. Clarify construct definitions, dimensionality, measurement traditions, overlaps, and discriminant boundaries.
2. Map each relevant theory's core mechanism, assumptions, level of analysis, focal constructs, predicted relationships, and known limitations.
3. Compare where theories complement, compete, nest, or operate at different levels or time scales.
4. Identify consistent findings, null results, sign reversals, nonlinearities, asymmetries, and context-dependent results.
5. Explain whether disagreement is likely theoretical, contextual, temporal, measurement-based, design-based, or estimation-based.
6. Distinguish absence of evidence from evidence of no relationship.
7. Rank research gaps by theoretical consequence, empirical uncertainty, and feasibility. Do not treat a new country, sector, method, or dataset as sufficient novelty by itself.

## Develop the research framework

1. Select the smallest coherent set of constructs needed to explain the focal outcome or phenomenon.
2. For every proposed arrow, state the theoretical mechanism, supporting evidence, temporal logic, level of analysis, and boundary conditions.
3. Add mediators only when they explain how or why, and moderators only when they explain when, where, or for whom. Do not stack fashionable variables without mechanism.
4. Identify rival explanations, reverse causality, omitted constructs, feedback loops, nonlinearities, and competing model structures.
5. Reject, redefine, or reposition a preselected variable when the literature does not support its proposed role. Explain the decision.
6. Compare at least one parsimonious model with plausible alternatives before recommending a final framework.
7. Draft propositions or hypotheses only when requested and only to the strength allowed by the evidence and design. Avoid causal wording unless identification can support it.

## Deliver

Adapt the depth to the request and return, as applicable:

1. Review objective, scope, assumptions, and review mode
2. Coverage status: platforms and dates searched, access level, screening stage completed, record counts when available, and unresolved verification
3. Search strategy, search log, inclusion logic, and coverage limitations
4. Construct-definition and foundational-theory map
5. Thematic synthesis of current evidence, contradictions, and boundary conditions
6. Evidence matrix and research-gap ranking
7. Recommended conceptual framework with an arrow-by-arrow rationale
8. Rival models and framework risks
9. Propositions or hypotheses with calibrated language
10. Verified reference list and a list of sources requiring further access
11. Suggested handoff to `q1-manuscript-writer`, `research-proposal-writer`, `strict-peer-reviewer`, `citation-integrity-auditor`, `panel-econometrics-auditor`, or `pls-sem-fsqca-reviewer` when the next task falls within those workflows

Use this skill when evidence discovery, theory synthesis, or framework construction is primary, even if the result will later become a manuscript section. Use `q1-manuscript-writer` after the evidence base and framework are fixed.
